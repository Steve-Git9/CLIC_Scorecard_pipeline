## Response to the previous evaluation

This proposal is submitted again after the evaluation of an earlier submission to this programme. The evaluators recognised the relevance of the idea but asked for a more focused consortium and a smaller request for funding. The consortium has taken this advice seriously, and the main changes are listed below.

- Lindmark (Lindmark Coastal Studies Institute), a research organisation with a focus on coastal observation methods, has withdrawn from the consortium. Its tasks are taken over by Eskeby (Eskeby Technical University), a university with a research profile in coastal observation methods. ·
- Kellmar (Kellmar Technologies Ltd) has merged with another company and now operates under the name Orrenfjord Marine Systems Ltd. Its role in the project has not changed. ·
- The budget has been reduced through a more focused division of tasks and through the change in the consortium. The new budget is presented in section 5.2, and the budget of the earlier submission is reproduced for reference in section 5.1. ·

Other parts of the proposal have been shortened and clarified in response to the comments of the evaluators, and the work plan now describes the contribution of each partner more clearly.

## Executive summary

MAROFLOW is a cooperation project under the programme Interreg Baltic Sea Region. It brings together seven partners from several countries around the sea basin to make observations of the coast and the sea easier to combine, share and use in regional planning.

Policy relevance is addressed by sharing results with authorities responsible for regional strategy. Short summaries explain what has been learned, which obstacles remain and what changes in rules or practice might help. By offering evidence in a form that decision makers can use, the project hopes to influence how the cooperation area plans for the coming years.

Gender balance and inclusion are considered in the choice of speakers, in the design of materials and in the formation of working groups. The partners aim for balanced teams and for communication that is accessible to people with different backgrounds and abilities. These principles are reviewed at the steering group so that they remain visible and do not fade during busy periods.

Coastal regions face pressures that do not stop at national borders. Shipping, tourism, fishing and energy production all draw on the same water, and decisions taken on a shore are felt across the water. A shared picture of what is happening at sea helps local planners to act earlier, to avoid conflicts between users and to explain their choices to residents.

Following the plan described in this document, the consortium has prepared a total funding request of EUR 3,207,500 to cover the work packages described in section 3.

Learning from experience is built into the way the project works. After each major activity the participants reflect on what went well, what did not and what should change. These short reviews are recorded and shared, and their lessons are fed into later planning. In this way the project grows more effective as it proceeds and leaves useful experience for others.

A common approach to data brings benefits that reach well beyond the project. When providers describe their observations in a consistent way, new users can find and trust them without lengthy negotiation. Researchers save time, companies can build new products on a stable foundation, and public authorities gain confidence that their decisions rest on comparable evidence from all parts of the region.

Exploitation is planned from the beginning rather than added at the end. The partners identify which results could be operated as services, which could be sold as products and which are best shared as open resources. For each of these paths they discuss responsibilities, costs and possible operators, so that decisions can be taken calmly while the work is still under way.

The work is organised in six work packages: COORD, BRIDGE, SENSE, MODEL, PILOT, SPREAD. Together they cover coordination, the linking of data providers, the installation of sensing nodes, shared data models, pilot operation and dissemination.

Good collaboration depends on shared working habits as much as on formal agreements. The partners have agreed on common tools for document sharing, version control and meeting notes, and they use plain language in all internal communication. New colleagues are introduced to these habits early, so that changes in personnel do not interrupt the flow of work.

Companies stand to gain from clearer access to data and from a larger pool of potential customers. Small firms in particular can use shared services to develop products that would be too costly to build alone. The project supports this by publishing interface descriptions, offering test environments and inviting companies to take part in the pilot activities.

Smaller coastal municipalities often lack the staff and tools to use scientific data in their daily planning. They need information that is timely, understandable and free of unnecessary technical detail. The project therefore designs its services around the questions that local planners actually ask, and it checks with them regularly whether the answers are useful in practice.

Dissemination reaches different audiences through different channels. Public authorities are addressed through workshops and short briefings, companies through demonstrations and trade events, researchers through papers and conferences, and the general public through plain language articles and web pages. Messages are adapted to each group, and the response of audiences is observed to improve later activities.

Equal participation across countries is a guiding principle. Meetings rotate between locations, documents are prepared in a language that all can follow, and tasks are distributed so that every region gains from the collaboration. This approach helps the partners to understand each other better and to treat the shared sea as a common responsibility rather than a collection of national concerns.

The consortium expects the results to be used by public authorities, companies and research communities, and it has planned the work so that users take part from the start.

## 1 Relevance and objectives

## 1.1 Context and rationale

The proposal MAROFLOW, Marine Observation Flows for Shared Coastal Planning, responds to the need for shared and reliable information about the sea and its coast. This section explains the relevance of the work, the objectives and the approach. It also describes the starting position of the proposed solution and the results that the consortium expects to reach.

Residents and visitors profit indirectly through safer waters, healthier coastal environments and better public information. Clear and trustworthy updates about conditions at sea help people make sensible choices, whether they are fishing, sailing or simply walking on the shore. The project therefore pays attention to how information is presented to the general public.

Cooperation around a shared sea depends on trust that is built slowly through repeated joint work. Partners learn how colleagues in neighbouring countries plan, decide and report, and they gradually adopt common habits. The project treats this learning as a result in its own right, because durable networks outlast any single funding period and give coastal communities a stable base for further common action.

The project is guided by a plain principle: results should be useful to people outside the consortium. Every task is therefore described in terms of what it gives to a user, and progress is judged by whether that benefit becomes real. This focus helps the partners to resist unnecessary complexity and to concentrate on work that matters.

Uptake depends on whether potential users see a clear benefit and can adopt the results without large investment. The project describes who the users are, what they need, what they already have and what stops them from changing. This understanding guides the design of services and the plans for training, support and long term operation.

The project aims to bridge the gap between research results and everyday use. Findings that remain in journals do little for a harbour manager facing a storm warning. By keeping users involved from the start and by testing in realistic conditions, the work turns laboratory knowledge into services that can be operated, maintained and improved by ordinary organisations.

Data handling follows clear rules from the first day. Each dataset has a named responsible partner, a description of its origin and quality, and a statement of who may use it and under which conditions. These records are kept alongside the data itself, which makes it far easier to share results with confidence and to answer questions from reviewers.

Testing is built into the plan rather than left to the end. Early versions of tools and services are tried by small groups of users, whose comments shape the next version. This cycle of building, trying and adjusting reduces the risk of delivering something that looks fine on paper but fails to meet real needs.

Every work package begins with a short planning phase in which the participants confirm their tasks, agree on the methods to be used and identify dependencies on other packages. The outcome is recorded in a brief plan that is shared with the whole consortium. Changes during the work are discussed at the steering group and reflected in the plan so that everyone works from the same picture.

Fieldwork is planned around the seasons and the availability of vessels, harbours and local staff. Where weather can cause delays, the schedule includes reserve periods so that a single storm does not disturb later tasks. The partners share equipment where possible, which lowers costs and brings together the practical experience of several teams.

The work plan is organised in packages that each deliver a clear result for users. Packages are connected through defined interfaces, so that the outputs of a package become the inputs of the next. This structure keeps the work understandable, makes progress easy to follow and allows the coordinator to spot delays soon after they appear.

Training is part of every package that introduces new tools or methods. Staff at partner organisations and at user organisations receive practical sessions and written guidance so that they can operate the results without help from the developers. ## 4 Impact and market uptake

## 4.1 Expected impact

The project is expected to bring benefits to public authorities, companies, research communities and residents. Section 4.2 describes the uptake of results on the market, and section 4.3 explains the planned dissemination and exploitation.

Environmental benefits are expected from better knowledge of conditions at sea. Early detection of changes in water quality, ice or currents allows authorities to react sooner and to limit harm. The project does not claim to solve environmental problems by itself, but it supplies information that makes targeted and proportionate action possible.

Innovation in public services rarely comes from a single organisation. It grows where researchers, companies and public bodies test ideas together in real settings and learn from what fails. The consortium was formed with this principle in mind, so that each partner contributes a distinct kind of knowledge and each result can be tried by the people who will eventually depend on it.

Many observation systems already exist, but they were built for different purposes and speak different technical languages. Their data often sits in separate archives with unclear conditions of use. The project starts from the view that the main obstacle is not a lack of measurements but a lack of practical ways to combine them, and it concentrates its effort on closing that gap.

Sustainability is considered in every part of the work plan. Equipment is chosen for long service life and low energy use, travel is kept to what the collaboration truly needs, and meetings are held remotely whenever the purpose allows. The partners also look at how services can continue after the funding period, so that benefits do not disappear when the project ends.

The project aims to bridge the gap between research results and everyday use. Findings that remain in journals do little for a harbour manager facing a storm warning. By keeping users involved from the start and by testing in realistic conditions, the work turns laboratory knowledge into services that can be operated, maintained and improved by ordinary organisations.

A common approach to data brings benefits that reach well beyond the project. When providers describe their observations in a consistent way, new users can find and trust them without lengthy negotiation. Uptake depends on whether potential users see a clear benefit and can adopt the results without large investment. The expected impact is felt first by those who plan and manage coastal areas. The partners expect that the project will encourage new joint studies, new students and new questions about how the sea and the coast interact.

Data handling follows clear rules from the first day. Each dataset has a named responsible partner, a description of its origin and quality, and a statement of who may use it and under which conditions. These records are kept alongside the data itself, which makes it far easier to share results with confidence and to answer questions from reviewers.

Training is part of every package that introduces new tools or methods. Staff at partner organisations and at user organisations receive practical sessions and written guidance so that they can operate the results without help from the developers. Feedback from these sessions is collected and used to improve both the tools and the instructions that accompany them.

Reporting is kept light but regular. Each partner provides a short account of progress, problems and plans at fixed intervals, and the coordinator combines these into an overview for the steering group. The aim is to give early warning of difficulties and to avoid long documents that few people read, while still creating a reliable record of what was done and why.

Every work package begins with a short planning phase in which the participants confirm their tasks, agree on the methods to be used and identify dependencies on other packages. The outcome is recorded in a brief plan that is shared with the whole consortium. Changes during the work are discussed at the steering group and reflected in the plan so that everyone works from the same picture.

Technical choices favour open standards and well documented interfaces. Open approaches make it easier for outside organisations to connect their own systems, they lower the cost of maintenance and they avoid dependence on a single supplier. Where a proprietary component cannot be avoided, the partners record the reason and describe how it could be replaced later.

Learning from experience is built into the way the project works. After each major activity the participants reflect on what went well, what did not and what should change. These short reviews are recorded and shared, and their lessons are fed into later planning. In this way the project grows more effective as it proceeds and leaves useful experience for others.

Technical choices favour open standards and well documented interfaces. Open approaches make it easier for outside organisations to connect their own systems, they lower the cost of maintenance and they avoid dependence on a single supplier. Where a proprietary component cannot be avoided, the partners record the reason and describe how it could be replaced later.

Data handling follows clear rules from the first day. Each dataset has a named responsible partner, a description of its origin and quality, and a statement of who may use it and under which conditions. These records are kept alongside the data itself, which makes it far easier to share results with confidence and to answer questions from reviewers.

Dependence on a single supplier is avoided. For critical equipment and services the partners identify alternatives in advance and keep contact with more than a single source.
